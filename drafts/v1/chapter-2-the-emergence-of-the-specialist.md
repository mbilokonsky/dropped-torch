## The Emergence of the Specialist

If the previous section described a *genetic substrate* — a distribution of cognitive styles created by archaic hybridization — this section asks what happened *culturally* when that substrate expressed itself in living communities. The answer turns out to be remarkably consistent, and remarkably strange.

Across virtually all documented hunter-gatherer societies, a specific social role emerges: a person who lives at the margins of the group, experiences consciousness differently than most, undergoes a devastating transformative crisis, and — if they survive it — becomes the community's specialist in navigating the invisible forces that shape collective life. In Siberia, this person is called a *shaman* (from the Tungusic *sâman*); in other cultures, the role goes by hundreds of different names. Whether you should use "shaman" as a cross-cultural category is itself a live scholarly debate we'll address below. But the *phenomenon* — the recurrence of this role across independent cultures on every inhabited continent — demands explanation.

---

### The Specialist's Requirements

Before we look at the ethnographic record, let's reason from first principles. If a human community were going to maintain a specialist whose job was to monitor the health of the collective, navigate domains of experience inaccessible to others, and deliver hard truths to the group — what *cognitive and perceptual traits* would that role select for?

**You'd need someone who can feel when systems are misaligned.** The specialist's core function is monitoring something cultures describe variously as "spiritual health," "balance," or "harmony" — the felt quality of collective life. This requires a sensorium that *registers* coherence and its absence, ideally at a level below conscious articulation. You'd want someone who walks into a gathering and *feels in their body* that something is wrong between two families, even before anyone has spoken about it. Conversely, you'd want someone who experiences genuine *pleasure* — a kind of frisson — when the systems they're embedded in are running cleanly. This person's nervous system isn't just processing information about the social and ecological environment; it's *evaluating* that information against some felt sense of how things ought to be.

Contemporary neuroscience has a name for this kind of processing, though it's usually framed as a clinical finding rather than a capacity: autistic individuals frequently show differences in interoception — the sense of the body's internal states — and in what researchers call "predictive processing," the brain's constant comparison of incoming sensory data against internal models.[^pellicano2012] When the models and the data match, the system is quiet. When they don't, the mismatch signal is intense, sometimes overwhelming. What the clinical literature describes as "sensory sensitivities" and "insistence on sameness" may be, viewed from a different angle, exactly the perceptual architecture you'd want in someone whose job is to detect when the world is out of tune.

This also illuminates what the autistic community has come to call "autistic joy" — that specific, almost electrical pleasure in the presence of pattern, symmetry, elegance, or coherence. If your nervous system is calibrated to detect systemic alignment, then *finding* that alignment should feel like something. It should feel like the thing that makes the whole enterprise worthwhile. For the specialist, that feeling isn't a quirky preference — it's the signal that tells them the community is well.

**You'd need someone the community can trust with unverifiable reports.** The specialist's entire value depends on credibility. They enter altered states, journey into the spirit world, encounter entities, and return with information the community is supposed to act on — information that *cannot be independently verified*. This is an enormous ask. The community is being told to move camp, or change leadership, or reconcile a conflict, on the word of someone describing experiences no one else can share.

The single most effective solution to this credibility problem isn't institutional — it's biographical. If the specialist is someone who has *demonstrably been unable to deceive* since childhood, the trust problem largely solves itself. Not someone who *chooses* honesty as a moral commitment (commitments can be abandoned), but someone for whom deception is effortful, unnatural, and visibly uncomfortable when attempted. Someone the village has watched tattle on themselves since they were three. Someone whose face betrays their internal state whether they want it to or not.

The well-documented autistic relationship to deception — not an incapacity to lie, but a deep reluctance, a felt wrongness about it, and a marked lack of facility when attempting it — isn't a social deficit in this context. It's a *credential*. It's the thing that makes the specialist's reports trustworthy precisely because they can't be faked.

**You'd need someone who enters altered states of consciousness naturally.** Shamanic practice worldwide relies on a consistent induction technology: rhythmic, repetitive sensory stimulation. Drumming, chanting, rocking, repetitive dancing — these are the universal tools for achieving the altered states in which the specialist does their work. Winkelman's neurotheological framework identifies these practices as producing integrative states through entrainment of brain oscillations.[^winkelman2000]

Now consider: rhythmic, repetitive, self-generated sensory-motor behavior is also a core feature of autistic experience. The clinical literature calls it "stereotypy" or "self-stimulatory behavior." Autistic people call it stimming. Either way, it involves the spontaneous production of exactly the kind of rhythmic sensory input that shamanic cultures systematically deploy for consciousness alteration. What if these aren't coincidentally similar? What if the person who has been rocking and humming since infancy — who finds that these behaviors regulate their nervous system, shift their attentional state, and produce a felt sense of integration — is exercising the same neural circuitry that, when culturally channeled, becomes the shaman's drum? The specialist doesn't need to *learn* rhythmic induction from scratch. They need someone to recognize what they're already doing and show them what to do once they get there.

**You'd need someone whose system can survive total overload and reconstitute.** This is perhaps the most important requirement, and the most painful to articulate. The shamanic initiatory crisis — that near-death, identity-dissolving, years-long ordeal that transforms the potential into the practitioner — *requires a nervous system that is capable of total catastrophic overwhelm followed by structural reorganization.* Not everyone's system can do this. For many people, that level of overload would produce damage without transformation, breakdown without reconstitution.

The autistic nervous system is documented to have exactly this capacity — or rather, exactly this *vulnerability that is also a capacity.* The meltdown/shutdown cycle, in which accumulated sensory or emotional overload produces a complete collapse of executive function followed by a period of withdrawal and gradual reconstitution, mirrors the formal structure of the initiatory crisis at every level: catastrophic overwhelm → dissolution of ordinary functioning → withdrawal from social life → slow reintegration as a changed system. Autistic people who have experienced severe burnout — the months- or years-long collapse that often follows sustained masking or environmental mismatch — describe a process that maps remarkably onto what Eliade documented:[^eliade1964] the old self dies, there is a period in which nothing works and nothing makes sense, and eventually something new emerges that was not there before.

The difference — and this is the crux of the entire essay — is not whether the crisis happens. It's whether there is *a cultural container for it.* In a society with shamanic infrastructure, the crisis is recognized as an initiation. There are elders who have survived the same thing. There is a framework that gives the suffering meaning. There is a role waiting on the other side. In a society without that infrastructure, the identical neurological event is a diagnosis, a disability, a problem to be medicated — or simply an inexplicable catastrophe that the person must survive alone.

### The Cross-Cultural Evidence

The empirical case for this role's recurrence is stronger than casual readers might expect. Michael Winkelman, in what remains the most rigorous quantitative study of the phenomenon, analyzed ethnographic data from a stratified 47-society subsample of the Standard Cross-Cultural Sample (SCCS), coding over two hundred variables relating to ritual procedures, induction practices, altered states of consciousness, and spiritual power relations.[^winkelman1992] His cluster analysis identified a specific practitioner type — the "shaman" — that appeared with remarkable consistency in foraging societies worldwide, and that was empirically distinguishable from healers, mediums, priests, and sorcerers found in more politically complex societies. This wasn't a vague "some cultures have spiritual people" finding; it was a statistically robust cross-cultural pattern with specific, recurring features.

Manvir Singh, publishing in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* in 2018 with commentary from 25 scholars across anthropology, philosophy, and psychology, offered a complementary (and partly competing) framework.[^singh2018] Singh proposed that shamanism is a set of cultural traditions that evolve because they adapt to universal features of human psychology — our tendency to seek explanations for unpredictable events, our susceptibility to displays of transformation, our need for specialists who can manage uncertainty. On Singh's account, shamanism recurs not because of a single spiritual reality, but because cultural evolution keeps rediscovering the same recipe for meeting the same psychological needs.

Both frameworks converge on a key point: this isn't an occasional curiosity. It's a *human universal* at the level of social organization, as reliable as kinship systems or language. As Winkelman wrote, shamanic practices reflect "underlying neurological processes" rooted in fundamental brain operations — what he calls "the original neurotheology."[^winkelman2004] The universals include altered states of consciousness, death-and-rebirth imagery, soul flight, animal transformation, and community healing rituals, all of which map onto identifiable neuropsychological processes.

Now: critics of the shamanism concept — most notably Alice Kehoe — argue that applying a Tungusic word to practices from Africa, Oceania, and the Americas flattens culturally specific traditions into a false universal.[^kehoe2000] This is a legitimate caution. Every culture that has this role has its own name for it, its own cosmology surrounding it, its own relationship to the practitioner. The Mapuche *machi*, the Yoruba *babalawo*, the Lakota *wičháša wakȟáŋ*, the Sámi *noaidi* — these are not interchangeable. But the same critique could be applied to words like "priest" or "king," which we freely use cross-culturally while acknowledging local variation. The question isn't whether every culture's version is identical — it's whether the *structural pattern* recurs, and the empirical answer to that is clearly yes.

For this essay, I'll use "shamanic specialist" or simply "the specialist" as a compromise — acknowledging both the reality of the cross-cultural pattern and the danger of collapsing distinct traditions into a single label.

### What the Specialists Had in Common

With those caveats established, here's what the ethnographic record consistently shows about these individuals across cultures — and note how closely it maps onto the requirements we derived from first principles above.

**They were identified early and set apart.** In many traditions, children with shamanic potential were recognized in childhood — through signs at birth, unusual dreams, behavioral differences, or family lineage — and given over to existing practitioners for training.[^eliade1964] [^walsh1994] This training could last years. The role was not self-selected; it was recognized by the community and confirmed by the spirits, or by whatever language the local tradition used to describe the forces that chose the practitioner. Crucially, shamanic *potential* was not the same as being called — because the call, when it came, was devastating. The community needed to prepare the child for a crisis that might or might not arrive, and that would be unsurvivable without preparation.

**They often experienced gender, social norms, and perception differently than most.** The ethnographic record is rich with examples of gender variance among shamanic specialists — not universally, but with striking frequency across independent cultures. The Two-Spirit traditions of numerous Indigenous North American nations, the gender-fluid *machi* of the Mapuche, the *chibados* of the Ndongo kingdom in Angola — in each case, individuals who crossed or transcended gender categories frequently occupied roles as spiritual specialists and healers.[^roscoe1996] [^bacigalupo2007] [^williams1986] In the Mapuche tradition, the *machi*'s gender-shifting practice is explicitly linked to the hermaphroditic flowers of the sacred *foye* tree — the capacity to move between categories is understood as *the source of healing power itself*, not as an incidental trait.

This is *not* the same as saying "all gender-variant people were shamans" or "all shamans were gender-variant" — both claims would be wildly overstated. But the recurring association between those who crossed social categories and those who served as intermediaries between worlds is too consistent to dismiss as coincidence. As anthropologist Ana Mariella Bacigalupo documented in fifteen years of field research, the specialist's power frequently derives precisely from their *liminality* — their location between and across categories that most people experience as fixed.[^bacigalupo2007]

**They underwent a devastating transformative crisis.** This is the most consistent feature across traditions. Eliade documented what he called the "shamanic initiatory crisis" — a life-or-death ordeal involving severe physical illness, psychological disintegration, withdrawal from community life, and experiences of symbolic death and dismemberment.[^eliade1964] The Haitian *manbo* who nearly died before accepting her calling and received instructions from the *lwa* in dreams.[^brown2001] The Siberian practitioner whose spirit helpers "dismembered" them and reconstituted their body with new organs. The Mexican healers who accepted their destinies after spiritual threats of death. The Tungus practitioner Chuonnasuan, whose initiatory illness stretched over years.

Turner and colleagues formalized this as the "shamanistic initiatory crisis" — a rite of passage commonly involving physical illness or psychological crisis that, crucially, was *not understood as pathology by the cultures in which it occurred*.[^turner1969] Modern cross-cultural scholarship generally rejects the earlier view that shamans were simply mentally ill.[^winkelman2010] In its cultural context, the initiatory crisis is a normative, even valued experience — a *sickness unto transformation* that produced, in those who survived it, individuals with capacities the community recognized as genuinely useful. The DSM-IV, notably, included "Religious or Spiritual Problem" (V62.89) as a non-pathological category partly in response to this anthropological evidence.

As we predicted in the requirements section: the crisis has a structure — calling, resistance, catastrophic breakdown, encounter with the numinous, and gradual reconstitution — that maps onto the autistic burnout cycle. But here it happens inside a container that gives it meaning.

**They served a specific function that cost the community resources to maintain.** This is the evolutionary puzzle that makes the entire pattern significant. In small-scale foraging societies where survival depends on efficient resource allocation, why would a community feed, protect, and grant authority to someone who often couldn't hunt or gather effectively, who lived at the social margins, who behaved in ways that transgressed norms?

The answer is that these specialists provided something the community couldn't get any other way: management of uncertainty. As both Winkelman and Singh emphasize from their different theoretical angles, the specialist's role centered on navigating domains where material knowledge was insufficient — illness with no obvious cause, hunting outcomes that couldn't be predicted, interpersonal conflicts whose roots were invisible, collective crises that required meaning-making to endure.[^winkelman2010] [^singh2018] The specialist reduced group anxiety around uncertain outcomes, facilitated social cohesion through ritual, and provided a framework for interpreting suffering that preserved community coherence.

Winkelman argues these functions were grounded in real neuropsychological processes — that the specialist's altered states produced genuine integrative healing responses, activating opioid-attachment mechanisms, social bonding pathways, and endogenous healing capacities.[^winkelman2004] The biological understandings of shamanic universals, as he put it, "indicate that these potentials are still part of human nature and have relevance for understanding contemporary human health and illness." Singh emphasizes the cultural-evolutionary pressures that selected for specialists who *appeared* able to control uncertain outcomes, regardless of whether they actually could.[^singh2018]

For our purposes, what matters is the convergence: both frameworks agree that the role persisted because it provided genuine adaptive value to the group, and that the *type of person* who filled this role had distinctive psychological and perceptual characteristics that were, in some fundamental sense, *built into the human distribution*.

**The specialist's authority was distinct from material leadership.** One pattern worth flagging for later: across many traditions, the shamanic specialist and the chief were separate roles, sometimes in creative tension. The specialist's authority came from their relationship to the invisible world; the chief's authority came from their capacity to organize material life. When these roles merged — when the person who could see the spirits also controlled the resources — the results were often described in traditional lore as dangerous, even catastrophic. We'll return to this in the next section.

### The Connections

Now step back and consider this pattern in light of what we established in the previous section.

We have a genetic substrate — created by archaic hybridization — that produces a distribution of cognitive styles in every human population. Some of these styles involve enhanced visual-spatial processing, reduced default mode network activity, heightened pattern recognition, diminished social-processing bandwidth, and a nervous system calibrated for intense mismatch detection. These are traits that contemporary neuroscience increasingly links to what we call neurodivergence.

And we have a cultural universal — the shamanic specialist — that appears independently in every documented foraging society, consistently characterized by perceptual difference, social marginality, gender variance, a devastating transformative crisis, and capacities that the community values enough to pay for.

We derived from first principles a set of cognitive requirements for this role — system-coherence detection, radical honesty as trust architecture, natural facility with rhythmic consciousness alteration, and the capacity to survive total overload and reconstitute — and found that these requirements map precisely onto traits the clinical literature documents in neurodivergent populations, typically framed as symptoms rather than capacities.

The argument is not that "shamans were autistic" — that flattens both categories beyond usefulness. The argument is that the same cognitive-perceptual distribution that we now label "neurodivergent" appears to be what human cultures have been *detecting and institutionalizing* for tens of thousands of years, under a thousand different names. That the deep-perceiving, pattern-sensitive, socially-atypical members of the group were not an accident or a pathology — they were a *recognized human type*, and cultures that built infrastructure to identify, support, and make use of them thrived.

The specialist didn't just happen to exist. The specialist was *cultivated*. Children were identified. They were trained. They were given a framework for understanding their difference. They were initiated through a crisis that, terrible as it was, had *structure* — elders who had survived the same thing, a community that expected and honored the outcome, a role waiting on the other side.

What happens when that infrastructure is destroyed?
