# A Circuitous, Speculative History of Neurodiversity, Spirit, and the Evolution of Consciousness

What follows is a fast and loose theory that seeks to stitch together facts, stories and inferences across a wide domain of human experience. This is a theory of neurodiversity, but it's one that begins in deep history and makes its point slowly, weaving together a number of domains that are normally considered out of scope for serious inquiry into this topic.

My intent here is not to provide a rigorous, well-cited argument that'll stand up to scrutiny on its own — my intent here is rather to tell a story that hopefully sparks the imagination of folks who are in a better position than I am to falsify and validate the various claims I'm going to make. As you'll see through the course of this essay, this type of "vibes based" approach to knowledge — as opposed to a materially grounded, fact-based, "objective" approach — is my attempt to not only describe but also embody a tradition that predates enlightenment or even imperial ways of generating knowledge.

We're going to touch on human evolution, consciousness, spirituality, empire, religion, art and the history of psychology before circling back around and sticking the landing here in 2026. The map I'm charting is rooted in associative reasoning, radical constructionism and speculative inference, and is — again — not intended to hold up on its own to scrutiny. But nor should it be possible to dismiss the points I'm making out of hand, and I'll consider this essay a success if it makes a skeptical reader even purse their lips in reflection.

So, without further ado, here we go.

## The Archaic Inheritance

Our story has to begin somewhere, so let's start with a question: what happened to the human brain when different species of humans started having children together?

It's now well established that *Homo sapiens* is not a "pure" lineage. As our ancestors moved through the world, they encountered and interbred with multiple populations of archaic humans — Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans across eastern Asia and into Oceania, and at least one "ghost population" in Africa whose fossils we haven't yet found but whose DNA persists in modern West African genomes.[^durvasula] The picture that's emerged over the last decade is one of repeated, widespread hybridization — not as a rare accident but as a recurring feature of human life across the entire planet for hundreds of thousands of years.

This matters for our story because of what these archaic cousins brought to the table cognitively.

Consider the Neanderthals. Their brains were, on average, slightly *larger* than ours — sometimes reaching 1600 cubic centimeters compared to the modern human average of about 1400. But the architecture was different. Neanderthals devoted proportionally more neural volume to visual processing and body control — larger occipital regions — at the expense of other areas,[^pearce2013] while *Homo sapiens* developed relatively larger cerebellar hemispheres linked to cognitive flexibility, language processing, and working memory.[^kochiyama] Neuroimaging studies of living humans who carry more Neanderthal DNA reveal increased functional connectivity in visual processing pathways and *decreased* connectivity in the default mode network — the brain system associated with social cognition, daydreaming, and self-referential thought.[^gregory2021] In plain language: Neanderthal brains appear to have been wired for deep perceptual attention at the expense of the social-processing networks that define the *Homo sapiens* cognitive style.

This cognitive profile shows up in the archaeological record. Neanderthals' signature technology, the Levallois technique, required mentally visualizing a finished tool's shape *before striking it from the stone* — a process demanding exceptional visual-spatial planning, precise motor control, and hierarchical reasoning.[^wynn2004] They manufactured birch bark adhesives requiring exact temperature control. They coordinated hunts that drove megafauna over cliffs using detailed knowledge of animal behavior and landscape. This was not crude or "primitive" intelligence — it was a *different kind* of intelligence, one optimized for deep environmental reading and precise material engagement rather than for the large-scale social coordination that characterized *Homo sapiens*.

And here's what makes all of this newly relevant: in 2024, researchers at Clemson University and Loyola University New Orleans published a landmark study in *Molecular Psychiatry* demonstrating that specific Neanderthal-derived genetic variants are significantly enriched in autistic individuals — not because autistic people carry *more* Neanderthal DNA overall, but because particular fragments affecting brain connectivity show up at higher rates.[^pauly2024] Twenty-five genetic markers linked to brain development were identified as more common in autistic people across three ethnic groups: Black non-Hispanic, white Hispanic, and white non-Hispanic. The brain connectivity patterns associated with these variants — heightened visual processing, reduced default mode network activity — closely mirror the cognitive profiles many autistic people report: keen pattern recognition, intense perceptual detail, and social fatigue.

Crucially, and this is where an earlier version of the "Neanderthal autism hypothesis" fell apart and had to be rebuilt: these findings are not confined to people of European descent.

For years, researchers assumed Neanderthal DNA was a European and Asian phenomenon, which made any link to autism — a condition found globally — seem geographically implausible. But a 2020 study from Princeton overturned that assumption, demonstrating that African populations also carry Neanderthal DNA, introduced through back-migrations of Eurasian populations over the past 20,000 years.[^chen2020] [^harris2023] As the researchers concluded, remnants of Neanderthal genomes survive in *every modern human population studied to date*. And the Neanderthal-autism link holds across ethnic lines — the Casanova study found enrichment in all three ethnic groups tested, though the specific variants differed by population, suggesting that the underlying susceptibility is shaped by interaction between archaic fragments and the broader background genome.

But Neanderthals are only part of the story. Denisovan DNA — concentrated in East Asian, South Asian, and Oceanian populations — has been linked to variants affecting brain development,[^yermakovich2024] and researchers have explicitly noted that Denisovan introgression may play a parallel role in autism susceptibility among populations with Asian and Native American ancestry.[^casanova_denisovan] A Denisovan-derived genetic variant affecting zinc regulation, identified by researchers at the Institute of Evolutionary Biology and Pompeu Fabra University, appears to have provided an evolutionary advantage for cold adaptation while simultaneously predisposing carriers to neuropsychiatric conditions.[^rocaumbert2023] Meanwhile, those ghost archaic populations in West Africa — species we can't even name yet, who diverged from our lineage over 360,000 years ago — contributed 2-19% of the genome of modern West African populations.[^durvasula] What cognitive traits those fragments carry remains unknown, but the principle is the same: hybridization reshuffled the neurological deck.

A parallel line of research tells a complementary story for ADHD. A 2020 genomic study published in *Scientific Reports* found that Neanderthal-introgressed alleles are enriched in ADHD risk variants, and that the frequency of ADHD-associated alleles has been steadily decreasing since Paleolithic times — consistent with the "mismatch theory" that traits once adaptive in ancestral environments became disadvantageous as human societies shifted toward agriculture and sedentism.[^estellercucala2020]

The emerging picture, then, is not "Neanderthals gave us autism." It's something broader and stranger: that the modern human brain is a *hybrid* organ, shaped by repeated interbreeding with multiple archaic species, each of which had evolved its own cognitive style over hundreds of thousands of years of independent adaptation. When those different cognitive architectures collided in the genome of a single species, the result wasn't a neat blend — it was a *distribution*. Most people ended up with a cognitive profile dominated by the *Homo sapiens* social-processing template. But some people inherited enough of the archaic pattern — whether Neanderthal, Denisovan, or something we haven't identified yet — that their brains organized differently. More visual, less social. More attentive to pattern and material detail, less tuned to the consensus frequencies of the group.

What we now call "neurodivergence" may be, in part, the living legacy of this hybridization — not a defect in the human design, but an echo of older ways of being human that persist in the genome because they were, and in many contexts still are, genuinely adaptive.

Put these points on the back-burner. We're preparing them for later use. But consider: what might it have been like for someone whose brain was organized along more archaic lines — oriented toward deep perception, material craft, and solitary environmental mastery — to be born into a culture increasingly optimized for large-scale social coordination, symbolic consensus, and group conformity?

Are you starting to get a sense of where this is going?
