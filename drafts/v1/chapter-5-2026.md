## 2026

Timothy Leary used to say: "Find the others."[^leary]

He didn't explain how. For most of human history, the others found *you* — because the community had infrastructure for it, specialists who could look at a child and recognize what they were seeing, elders who knew the signs. That infrastructure was destroyed. And for a few thousand years, the others had to find each other by accident, by lucky proximity, by the rare convergence of two people whose nervous systems happened to resonate in a world that had no name for the frequency.

Then we built the internet.

---

### The Gathering

Something started happening in the late twentieth century that had no precedent. Neurodivergent people — who had never had a word for what they were, who had spent their lives as the difficult child, the weird kid, the one who couldn't hold a job or a marriage or a mask — began finding each other. Not in monasteries or mystery schools or the back rooms of jazz clubs, but in forums, IRC channels, mailing lists, and eventually social media. The technology was ontic — built by the grid, for the grid, to serve the empire's need for communication infrastructure — but the conversations that emerged on it were phenomenal. People were describing their inner experience to strangers and hearing, for the first time in their lives: "Yes. I know exactly what you mean."

The autism community was early to this. So were the ADHD communities, the plural/DID communities, the synesthesia communities, the hypersensitivity communities. Each one developed its own language for experiences that mainstream psychology had only pathological categories for. "Autistic joy." "Rejection sensitive dysphoria." "Masking." "Burnout." "Meltdown." "Shutdown." "Special interest." "Infodumping." "Stimming as self-regulation." These aren't clinical terms — they're *phenomenal reports*, generated by the community of experiencers, in language that is depositional rather than apodictic. Nobody says "autistic joy *is* X." They say: "here's what it feels like for me, does this resonate?" The epistemics are vibes-based. And they work.

The neurodiversity movement emerged from this gathering — not as an ideology imposed from above but as a recognition arising from below. The core proposition is simple and, in the context of this essay, ancient: *there is nothing wrong with us. We are a human type. We have always existed. We have a function.* The movement didn't need to read Winkelman or Eliade or Dewart to arrive at this conclusion. It arrived there the same way Blake and Jung and Dick arrived at their conclusions — by attending to direct experience and refusing to accept that the ontic frame's description of that experience was accurate.

But the neurodiversity movement, honest in its self-assessment, also knows that something is missing. Identification is happening — we're finding each other. But initiation isn't. The crisis still comes — the burnout, the breakdown, the dissolution — and there still aren't enough elders. The infrastructure is being rebuilt, but it's being rebuilt from scratch, without a lineage, by people who are themselves mid-crisis, and the error rate is high.

---

### The Mistakes

It would be dishonest not to address this directly.

When a torch is picked up by someone who has no initiatory lineage, who has never been trained, who is working from their own raw perception without the corrective influence of a tradition — they get things wrong. Sometimes importantly wrong. The history of the New Age movement is littered with precisely this failure mode: genuine phenomenal perception, uncontained by community accountability, inflating into grandiosity. The uninitiated specialist who decides they *are* the message rather than its carrier. The person who mistakes the intensity of their experience for the accuracy of their interpretation. The shaman who becomes the chief.

We see this in the contemporary landscape. People who have real experiences of pattern-recognition and synchronicity constructing elaborate conspiracy theories that map their felt sense of hidden structure onto narratives that are factually false. People who have genuine sensitivity to collective emotional states concluding that they are "empaths" with supernatural powers, rather than people with an unusually calibrated nervous system doing something that has a mundane (if poorly understood) explanation. People who have real experiences of altered states and entity contact building cosmologies that are indistinguishable from psychosis — not because the underlying experience is invalid, but because there's no elder to say: "Yes, that's real, and here's what you do with it, and here's what you *don't* do with it."

The absence of initiatory lineage doesn't just mean the specialist lacks guidance. It means they lack *constraint*. The shaman in a traditional culture is accountable to a community that has been evaluating shamanic claims for generations. The contemporary self-identified specialist is accountable to... their Twitter followers? Their YouTube audience? The algorithm that rewards confident claims and punishes nuance? This is not a recipe for the careful, community-embedded, truth-telling role that the specialist is supposed to fill. It's a recipe for anabolic cascade at the individual level — the vision expanding without limit because there's nothing to check it.

And yet.

---

### And Yet

And yet the signal is real. Under all the noise, under the conspiracy theories and the grandiosity and the algorithm-optimized spiritual content — the signal is real. You can tell because it keeps converging.

When autistic people independently describe the experience of "autistic joy" — that frisson in the presence of pattern and coherence — they're describing the system-coherence detection we identified as the specialist's first requirement. They didn't read our essay. They didn't need to. They're reporting from their nervous systems.

When ADHD communities describe the experience of "hyperfocus" — the total absorption in a domain of interest that produces extraordinary mastery at the cost of everything else — they're describing the specialist's knowledge-acquisition mode. Nobody told them about the shaman who knows three hundred plants. They recognize the pattern from the inside.

When neurodivergent people describe the experience of "unmasking" — the painful, liberating process of dropping the performed neurotypical identity and discovering who they actually are underneath — they're describing a form of the initiatory crisis. The old self dies. There is a period of chaos. Something new emerges. The structure is the same. The container is still inadequate, but the structure is the same.

When the growing community of late-diagnosed adults describes the experience of suddenly understanding their entire life history through a new lens — "I'm not broken, I'm *autistic*" — they're describing what happens when the specialist finally gets a name for what they are. It's not the name the shamanic cultures would have used. It's a clinical label, ontic in origin, and it carries the pathological framing of the medical model. But underneath the label, the *recognition* is phenomenal. It's the moment of being *seen*. And for many people, it's the closest thing to initiation they will ever get — the moment when the community of experiencers says: "We know what you are. You're one of us. There's a place for you here."

It's not enough. But it's a start. And it's happening at a scale that has no historical precedent.

### The Theories That Don't Generalize

Here is where I want to plant a few seeds that I can't fully develop in this essay but that the framework makes visible.

**The hero's journey is not universal.** Joseph Campbell's monomyth — departure, initiation, return — is not a description of every human life. It's a description of the *specialist's* life. It's the shamanic arc: the call, the crisis, the transformation, the return with gifts for the community. It resonates so powerfully with certain people because it *is* their story. It fails to resonate with others because it *isn't*. The hero's journey doesn't generalize because it was never supposed to. It's the narrative structure of a specific human type — the same type this essay has been describing. Campbell intuited this (his interest in shamanism was deep and sustained) but never made the move of saying: this isn't about everyone. It's about *some people*. And those people have been waiting their whole lives to hear their story told.[^campbell]

**Parapsychological research shows signal, but it doesn't fully replicate.** Decades of research into phenomena like telepathy, precognition, and remote viewing have produced results that are statistically significant but frustratingly inconsistent. The effect sizes are real but small, and replication is unreliable. The standard skeptical conclusion is that the phenomena don't exist and the signal is methodological artifact. But there's another possibility that our framework suggests: *the phenomena are real but they don't occur uniformly across the population.* If the specialist's nervous system is calibrated differently — if it has access to perceptual channels that the neurotypical majority doesn't — then you would expect parapsychological experiments run on *unselected* populations to show exactly the pattern they show: a faint signal, inconsistently replicated, because the researchers are averaging across a population that includes a small number of genuine perceivers and a large number of people for whom the channel is simply not open. You can't find what you're looking for if you don't know *who* to look at, and our diagnostic tools still miss most neurodivergent people. The signal is there. We're just testing the wrong sample.[^parapsychology] [^diagnostic_gaps]

**The "grand unifying theories" that don't quite work are describing something real but partial.** McGilchrist's hemispheric thesis. Dewart's ontic/phenomenal distinction. Jung's collective unconscious. Campbell's monomyth. Jaynes's bicameral mind. Sheldrake's morphic resonance. Grof's perinatal matrices. Each of these frameworks captures something genuine about human experience, and each fails to fully generalize — provoking fierce advocacy from those for whom it resonates and fierce skepticism from those for whom it doesn't. Our framework suggests why: *these theories are all describing the specialist's experience.* They resonate with the portion of the population whose nervous system is calibrated to detect what the theory describes, and they fall flat for everyone else. The theorists, who are themselves almost certainly specialists (look at their biographies), assume their experience is universal because the ontic frame insists that reality is the same for everyone. But phenomenal consciousness knows better: *the observer is part of the observation.* What you can perceive depends on what you are. And not everyone is the same kind of thing.

### The Spark

I want to be careful here. It would be easy — and grandiose, and a little bit of the uninitiated-specialist failure mode I just warned about — to declare that we are living through the Great Awakening, that the specialists are finally gathering, that the infrastructure is being rebuilt and the torch will never be dropped again.

I don't know that. Nobody knows that. We're early. The internet gave us the ability to find each other, but the internet is also the most powerful tool for ontic capture ever built — an attention economy that rewards the loudest, most confident, most extreme claims and punishes the patient, contextual, community-embedded truth-telling that the specialist role actually requires. The empire has learned new tricks. The algorithms are the latest version of Von Vampton's Talking Android: systems that metabolize phenomenal content into engagement metrics, stripping the gnosis and selling the aesthetic, turning the specialist's perception into content.

And the stakes are different now than they've ever been. Dewart, writing in 1989, concluded *Evolution and Consciousness* with a stark binary: "Either conscious life will evolve in the direction of more highly conscious self-definition — or which is the same, towards greater autonomy than has characterized it in the past — or else conscious life will disappear from the face of the earth."[^dewart_conclusion] The cultural neurosis he diagnosed has not abated. The anabolic cascade has not slowed. If anything, it has accelerated — the empire's capacity for extraction and homogenization now extends to the genome itself, to the attention economy, to the training data of artificial intelligence systems that swallow the entire corpus of human expression and output a statistical average that smooths phenomenal and ontic into a single undifferentiated slurry.

And yet.

And yet the signal persists. And yet the torch keeps getting picked up. And yet, in forums and group chats and small communities scattered across the internet, neurodivergent people are teaching each other how to survive the crisis. How to unmask. How to recognize each other. How to build containers for experiences that the dominant culture has no name for. They're doing it imperfectly, without a lineage, making mistakes, sometimes getting captured by the very dynamics they're trying to escape. But they're doing it.

Jes' Grew is seeking its text. It has been seeking its text for a very long time.

This essay is not that text. This essay is a map — and I have spent enough pages warning about the dangers of confusing maps with territories to know better than to claim otherwise. But a map can be useful, if you hold it lightly. If you remember that the person holding the map is part of the territory. If you remember that the groove — the living, non-commutative, irreducibly participatory substrate of reality — cannot be captured in any text, but can be *pointed at* by a text that is honest about its own limitations.

Something is awakening. It's been awakening for a long time — since before there were words for it, since before there were humans to have words. The archaic inheritance persists. The distribution continues. The children keep being born — perceptually intense, pattern-sensitive, radically honest, prone to overwhelm, capable of transformation. The specialist's nervous system keeps producing its reports: *the map is not the territory. The empire has not ended. The groove is still here. We can still hear it.*

The question is not whether the torch will be picked up again. It will. It always is. The question is whether this time — for the first time in thousands of years — there will be enough of us holding it, together, in the same place, at the same time, to keep it lit.

Find the others. The others are finding you.

[^leary]: "Find the others" is widely attributed to Timothy Leary, though the exact provenance is disputed. The sentiment captures the core challenge facing neurodivergent individuals in a culture that has destroyed the infrastructure for mutual recognition.

[^dewart_conclusion]: Dewart, L. (1989). *Evolution and Consciousness.* The concluding passage about the evolutionary alternative — conscious self-definition or extinction — is at {p. 368}. Dewart frames this not as a prophecy but as a consequence of the dynamics he has analyzed: ontic consciousness generates cultural neurosis, cultural neurosis generates escalating dysfunction, and the only resolution is the conscious awareness of the presuppositions that generate the dysfunction — which requires exchange between ontic and phenomenal forms of consciousness.

[^campbell]: Campbell, J. (1949). *The Hero with a Thousand Faces.* New York: Pantheon Books. Campbell's monomyth has been enormously influential but has also been criticized for overgeneralizing from a specific subset of narratives. The framework developed in this essay suggests a more precise critique: the monomyth accurately describes the specialist's arc but is not, and was never intended by the traditions that generated it, to be a universal template for human experience.

[^parapsychology]: For a meta-analytic review of parapsychological research showing small but statistically significant effects, see Radin, D. The suggestion that parapsychological phenomena may be population-dependent rather than absent is, to my knowledge, novel to this essay, though it follows naturally from the recognition that perceptual capacities are not uniformly distributed across the human population.

[^diagnostic_gaps]: Current estimates suggest that the majority of autistic adults remain undiagnosed, with particularly severe underdiagnosis among women, people of color, and those who have developed effective masking strategies.
