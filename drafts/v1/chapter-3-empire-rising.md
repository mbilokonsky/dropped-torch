## Empire Rising

One piece of wisdom you'll encounter across shamanic traditions, phrased differently but pointing at the same thing, is this: *the specialist must never become the chief.*

The Lakota distinguish the *wičháša wakȟáŋ* (holy man) from the *itȟáŋčhaŋ* (leader). The Mapuche keep the *machi* separate from the *lonko*. Across Siberia, the shaman advises but does not rule. This isn't incidental to how these cultures organized themselves — it's load-bearing. The specialist's power derives from their access to the invisible world; the chief's authority derives from their capacity to organize the visible one. When those functions are held by separate people, each constrains the other. The chief cannot ignore the spiritual health of the community because the specialist will call it out. The specialist cannot impose their visions unilaterally because the chief controls the resources.

But when the two roles collapse into one person — when the one who sees the spirits also commands the warriors — something goes wrong. Traditional lore across multiple cultures describes this scenario as an *abomination*, a violation of a structural principle that keeps the community sane. And if we look at the historical record with this frame in mind, a troubling pattern emerges: the great conquerors of history — Alexander, Caesar, Genghis Khan, Napoleon — were, almost without exception, individuals whose biographical details suggest exactly the kind of visionary intensity, obsessive drive, pattern-sensitivity, and disregard for social consensus that this essay has been describing as the specialist's cognitive profile. They weren't typical people who happened to acquire power. They were *atypical* people — quite possibly the very type that shamanic cultures would have identified as specialist material — who were never initiated, never given a container for their difference, and instead channeled it into material domination.

The warning embedded in shamanic tradition, then, isn't mystical — it's structural. When the person who can see the deeper patterns also has the power to *impose* those patterns on material reality, the result is a specific and recognizable pathology: the specialist's vision, ungrounded by community accountability, becomes a template that the world is forced to conform to. The map replaces the territory. The vision devours the world it was supposed to serve.

The name for the institutional form of this pathology is *empire*.

### The Map That Eats the Territory

Empire is not merely a large political entity. Plenty of large-scale human organizations — trade networks, language families, cultural spheres of influence — have existed without the specific dynamic we're describing here. Empire, in the sense that matters for this argument, is a *cognitive event* as much as a political one: the moment when a single model of reality — one culture's map — is imposed on the territory of lived human diversity, and the imposers lose the ability to distinguish between the two.

Several thinkers have converged on this insight from different angles. I want to trace three of them, because their convergence from such different starting points strengthens the case that we're looking at something real.

**Leslie Dewart** was a Canadian philosopher who, in *Evolution and Consciousness* (1989), proposed a radical theory of how human consciousness originates and develops.[^dewart1989] His central thesis is that consciousness is not genetically inherited but is *generated by speech* — literally, not metaphorically. Learning to speak doesn't express pre-existing conscious thoughts; it is the mechanism through which the human organism *acquires* the ability to experience consciously at all. Speech is therefore not just a tool for communication but the reproductive mechanism of human nature itself — the means by which each generation transmits consciousness to the next.

This is a bigger claim than the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — it's not that language *shapes* thought, but that speech *generates* conscious experience. Every conscious experience includes, within itself, awareness that the experience is occurring — what Dewart calls *self-presence*. This self-presence is not inborn (evidence from feral children demonstrates its complete absence in organisms raised outside human speech communities) but acquired through the process of learning to use signs assertively — to not merely produce communicative signals, but to *mean what you communicate*.{pp. 39, 167-174}

What makes Dewart essential for our argument is his analysis of what happened when thematic speech — the narrative, categorical speech that generates self-defining consciousness — emerged in *variant forms*. Dewart identifies two fundamental types, distinguished by a grammatical feature that seems trivial but carries enormous consequences: whether or not the language uses a verb "to be" functioning as both copula and existential indicator.

Languages that use verbal predication and verbs "to be" — found confidently only in Indo-European languages and Sumerian — produce what Dewart calls *apodictic* speech. When a speaker says "the apple *is* red," the grammar encodes an assumption: that the relationship between the apple and redness exists *out there*, in the apple, independent of the speaker's experience. The speaker's own assertive role in the act of experiencing is projected onto reality itself. Reality is conceived as having an inner structure — being, essence, substance — that speech merely mirrors.{pp. 260-262}

Dewart calls the consciousness generated by this form of speech *ontic consciousness*. It conceives reality as "being," causality as necessitation (a *force* that compels effects), and purposes as externally prescribed — by nature, by God, by law. The ontic mind experiences itself as receiving the world's assertions about itself, rather than as asserting the world into meaningful experience.{pp. 308-342}

Languages that lack copulative verbs — which is to say, *most of the world's languages* — produce what Dewart calls *depositional* speech. The depositional speaker remains aware of their own assertive role in the act of experiencing. They don't project their assertiveness onto reality. They don't confuse their map with the territory. Dewart calls the consciousness generated by this variant *phenomenal consciousness*.{p. 262}

Here is the crucial move: ontic consciousness is not a higher or more advanced form of consciousness. It is a *defective* one. It arises from what Dewart calls *absentmindedness* — a deficit in the self-presence of conscious experience, wherein consciousness cannot easily disentangle what belongs to its objects and what belongs to itself as an act. The ontic speaker, unable to fully perceive their own assertive role in experience, projects that assertiveness outward and concludes that reality itself "asserts" itself to the mind. This is the fundamental mechanism of projection — and it is built into the grammar of every sentence an Indo-European speaker utters.{pp. 83-87}

The consequences are staggering. Dewart observes that the Indo-European peoples have been "extraordinarily violent peoples, and the most successful, bloodthirsty, world-empire builders recorded in history," and that almost all phenomenal civilizations "perished at the hand of ontic conquerors."{p. 305} This is not, for Dewart, a coincidence. The ontic mind conceives causality as necessitation — as *force compelling effects* — because it projects the assertiveness of consciousness onto reality. A consciousness that understands causality as force naturally produces cultures organized around the application of force. And the ontic mind's power over reality perpetually outstrips its capacity for self-understanding, because the very mechanism that gives it power (projection, abstraction, model-building) is the same mechanism that blinds it to its own nature. Dewart calls this dynamic *cultural neurosis*: self-perpetuating patterns of collective self-defeating behavior, directly analogous to individual neurosis — a situation where "the quantity of efficient causality that human beings can command at any given time bears no necessary correlation with the quality of their lives."{pp. 302-303, 357}

**Iain McGilchrist**, in *The Master and His Emissary* (2009), arrives at a strikingly complementary framework through the neuroscience of hemispheric lateralization.[^mcgilchrist2009] The brain's right hemisphere, McGilchrist argues, attends to the world as a living, contextual, irreducibly complex whole — present, embodied, relational. The left hemisphere abstracts, categorizes, and manipulates *representations* of that world — it creates maps. The healthy relationship between the two is asymmetric: the right hemisphere's broad, contextual understanding should *govern* the left hemisphere's focused, manipulative capacity. But the left hemisphere, precisely because it deals in explicit models and categories, has the tools to *suppress* the right hemisphere's contributions — and once it does, it creates a self-reinforcing loop. It constructs a closed system of thought, it defines success by its own criteria, and it cannot even perceive what it's missing.

McGilchrist traces this dynamic through Western cultural history, identifying periods of relative hemispheric balance (Periclean Athens, the Renaissance) and periods of left-hemisphere dominance (late Rome, the Enlightenment, the present). His thesis is that modern Western civilization represents an advanced stage of what we might call *left hemisphere capture* — a world increasingly organized around abstractions that have lost contact with the reality they were supposed to represent.

The convergence with Dewart is remarkable — and not, I think, coincidental. McGilchrist's "left hemisphere capture" and Dewart's "ontic consciousness" are describing the same pathology from different angles — one neurological, the other linguistic-philosophical. Both identify a mode of cognition that mistakes its own models for reality. Both trace this mistake to a specific failure of self-awareness. And both argue that this failure has driven the distinctive pathologies of Western civilization. Dewart even anticipates McGilchrist's neurological frame: his concept of "absentmindedness" is, functionally, a description of what happens when the self-monitoring capacity of consciousness (which we might associate with right-hemisphere contextual awareness) fails, allowing the model-building capacity (which we might associate with left-hemisphere abstraction) to operate without constraint.

**The holonic metabolism framework** provides a third angle — one that abstracts the pattern into a domain-general principle.[^anabolic] In an essay I published earlier this year, I proposed that any dynamic system — a "holon," something that is simultaneously a whole in itself and a part of a larger whole — maintains its integrity through the coupling of *anabolic* processes (building up, accumulating, composing) and *catabolic* processes (breaking down, releasing, decomposing). When these processes are coupled and responsive to each other, the system exhibits *metabolic integrity* — a dynamic balance that allows it to adapt. When the processes become *decoupled* — when one subprocess operates independently of systemic regulation — the result is *metabolic cascade*.

An *anabolic cascade* is what happens when the building-up process escapes its regulatory constraints: growth becomes self-reinforcing and unresponsive to signals that should slow or stop it. In a cell, this produces a tumor. In a psyche, it produces narcissistic grandiosity — an identity that centers and protects itself against all external signals. In a culture, it produces empire — a social structure that "asserts itself over its neighbors and rivals, generally by force or more subtle means of coercion" and that "requires constant expansion in order to maintain its integrity." And crucially, the anabolic cascade in one holon *induces a compensatory catabolic cascade in adjacent holons*: the tumor induces cachexia in the body; the empire induces cultural dissolution in the peoples it conquers. The conquered people don't just lose territory — they lose the capacity for independent self-definition. They enter catabolic cascade in the domain of meaning.

All three frameworks converge on a single diagnosis: empire is what happens when a cognitive process that was supposed to *serve* the living whole instead *captures* it. The model-building, the abstraction, the map-making — these are genuinely useful capacities. They become pathological only when they escape their regulatory context. When the map-maker forgets they are making maps. When the emissary forgets he serves a master. When the left hemisphere suppresses the right. When apodictic speech projects its own assertiveness onto reality and concludes that what it has asserted is what *is*. When the anabolic process decouples from the catabolic and begins consuming everything in service of its own continuation.

And when the person who has these capacities in their most intense form — our specialist, our neurodivergent visionary — is given command of an army instead of a drum, the result is the structural catastrophe that shamanic traditions warned about: the vision that should have served the community instead devours it.

### How Empire Kills the Shamans

Empire, then, is a specific socio-cultural process that seeks to overwrite the natural heterogeneity of human cultures with copies of a single template — a template that is itself a reflection of the worldview of the empire's founders. It's the anabolic cascade made manifest in social organization: a single culture's model of reality expanding without limit, consuming the diversity it encounters, unable to recognize the diversity as anything other than raw material for its own continuation.

In practice, empires spread through a process that looks remarkably consistent across history and geography:

**First, you conquer.** This part is well-understood and needs little elaboration. Military force, economic pressure, or some combination. The material infrastructure of the target culture is brought under imperial control.

**Then, you make "small" alterations.** And this is where the relevant damage is done. The empire doesn't typically attempt to erase every aspect of the conquered culture — that would be too expensive and would provoke too much resistance. Instead, it targets specific structural elements, the ones that would allow the conquered culture to regenerate its own coherence. In the holonic metabolism frame, the empire performs a *targeted catabolic intervention* — it breaks down the specific structures that enable the conquered culture to maintain its independent metabolic integrity. High on this list, consistently, across empires from Rome to Spain to the British to the Soviet Union: *kill the shamans.*

This is not metaphorical. The historical record is explicit. When the Spanish conquered the K'iche' Maya in 1524, they specifically targeted the *ajtz'ib' ajk'ot* — the young literate keepers of spiritual and historical knowledge — kidnapping them into churches for forced conversion, separating them from the elders who could transmit their tradition. In Siberia, the Soviet state systematically persecuted shamans as "class enemies" and practitioners of superstition, imprisoning and executing them through the 1930s.[^balzer1999] Across colonial Africa, spirit mediums were executed or silenced, sacred objects destroyed, and entire cosmologies delegitimized.[^comaroff1991] The boarding school systems imposed on Indigenous peoples in North America and Australia explicitly separated children from their families and communities precisely to sever the transmission of spiritual knowledge between generations — to break what Dewart would recognize as the sociocultural matrix, the community of speakers through which consciousness and cultural identity reproduce themselves.{p. 213}[^burnette2023]

The pattern is consistent because the logic is consistent. The specialist is dangerous to the empire not because they have military power — they almost never do — but because they maintain the community's capacity for autonomous meaning-making. In Dewart's terms, the specialist maintains a *phenomenal* relationship to reality — one that is aware of its own constructedness, that holds its maps as maps, that keeps the catabolic check on the anabolic process. As long as the specialist is alive and functioning, the conquered culture can regenerate its own self-definition. Remove the specialist, and you remove the community's capacity for independent meaning. After that, ontic doctrine can flow into the void.

**Finally, you replace the organic tradition with imperial infrastructure.** Shamanic potentials — children who would have been identified by the old system and given over to specialists for training — now have nobody to identify them and no tradition to be initiated into. The community's spiritual needs don't disappear, but the people equipped to address them are gone. Into this vacuum steps the *priest* — not the local, organic, community-accountable practitioner with a personal relationship to the spirit world, but the representative of an institutional doctrine that serves the empire's need for standardized, controllable spiritual administration.

Winkelman's cross-cultural analysis is directly relevant here: his research demonstrated that the shaman, as a specific practitioner type, is associated with foraging societies, and that as political complexity increases (agriculture, states, empires), the shaman is replaced first by the "shaman/healer," then by the "healer," and finally by the "priest."[^winkelman1992] This trajectory — documented as an empirical pattern across dozens of societies — is precisely the imperial replacement dynamic: the specialist is progressively stripped of their autonomous relationship to the spirit world and subordinated to institutional authority. The *phenomenal* practitioner gives way to the *ontic* administrator.

This is the moment when animism gives way to what we now call "religion" — though that's a distinction we'll need to explore more carefully in the next section. For now, the key point is this: the transition from diverse, locally-rooted animist practice to standardized imperial religion is not an evolution. It's a *conquest*. It's Dewart's ontic consciousness overwriting phenomenal consciousness. It's McGilchrist's emissary usurping the master. It's an anabolic cascade in the domain of meaning-making, inducing compensatory catabolic cascade in every culture it touches.

### What Gets Lost

To understand the scale of what empire destroys, recall the architecture we described in the previous section. In a functioning culture with specialist infrastructure, neurodivergent individuals are:

- **Identified** in childhood by people who recognize their traits
- **Raised** within a framework that gives their difference a name and a purpose
- **Trained** in techniques that channel their natural capacities toward community benefit
- **Initiated** through a crisis that has structure, mentorship, and a role waiting on the other side
- **Integrated** as valued members of the community with a specific and irreplaceable function

When empire destroys the specialist infrastructure, *every element of this architecture collapses simultaneously*. The neurodivergent child is still born — the genetic substrate hasn't changed; the hybridization that created the distribution is hundreds of thousands of years old. But now:

- Nobody **identifies** them, because the people who knew what to look for are dead or silenced
- Nobody **raises** them with a framework for their difference, because the framework has been replaced by imperial doctrine that has no category for them — or worse, has a category that pathologizes them
- Nobody **trains** them, because the techniques have been lost or driven underground
- When the crisis comes — and it still comes, because it's neurological, not cultural — there is no **initiation container** for it. No elders. No framework. No role on the other side. Just a person in agony, alone with an experience that their culture cannot name
- And so they are not **integrated** but instead *pathologized*, *medicated*, *excluded*, or simply *broken*

The specialist's capacity doesn't disappear. The distribution persists. In every generation, in every population, children are born with the same perceptual intensity, the same system-coherence sensitivity, the same radical honesty, the same facility for altered states, the same nervous system that can be overwhelmed and reconstituted. But the cultural technology for *recognizing and channeling those capacities* has been systematically destroyed — not once, but repeatedly, across thousands of years, by wave after wave of imperial expansion.

And each wave is harder to recover from than the last, because each one removes not only the living practitioners but also the *memory* that such practitioners once existed. After enough generations, the conquered culture doesn't even know what it's lost. The people who would have been specialists are simply "difficult children," "mentally ill adults," "weird," "too sensitive," "unable to function." The infrastructure that once supported them becomes, at best, a folk memory — at worst, nothing at all.

### The Conqueror's Blindness

There's a final twist to this dynamic that's worth making explicit, because it will matter for the rest of the essay.

The empire doesn't just destroy the specialist infrastructure of conquered cultures. It also — necessarily, by its own logic — destroys *its own*. Dewart's analysis makes this inescapable: ontic consciousness is not something the Indo-European peoples *chose*. It's a property of the form of speech they acquired — a consequence of a deficit in self-presence that became encoded in grammar and transmitted to every subsequent generation through the sociocultural matrix.{pp. 262-263} The ontic mind cannot recognize the specialist's phenomenal perception as valuable, because it cannot recognize phenomenal perception at all. It has only one category for the specialist's insistence that the map isn't the territory: *wrong*.

The same left-hemisphere capture, the same ontic fixation, the same anabolic cascade that drives imperial expansion *also* makes it impossible for the imperial culture to recognize or support its own neurodivergent members. The emperor's culture has the same distribution of cognitive styles as every other human population. But the imperial template — the map that has replaced the territory — has no slot for the specialist. The imperial worldview *is* the worldview of the standardized, socially-coordinated, consensus-reality-maintaining cognitive majority. The specialist's perception — the one that sees the pattern behind the pattern, that feels the misalignment, that cannot be deceived or co-opted — is not just unnecessary to the imperial project. It's *threatening* to it. The specialist is a walking falsification of the premise that the map is the territory, and the empire cannot tolerate that.

And so the empire produces a peculiar irony: the very cognitive type that traditional cultures identified as essential to collective wellbeing is, in the imperial context, systematically identified as defective. The person who would have been the village's most valued spiritual resource becomes, in the imperial frame, the person who "doesn't fit in." The child whose perceptual intensity would have been recognized as a gift becomes the child who "can't sit still," "doesn't make eye contact," "takes things too literally," "has meltdowns."

The shaman sickness still comes. But now it's called a "nervous breakdown," or "burnout," or "treatment-resistant depression," or — in the most generous contemporary framing — "autistic burnout." And the person experiencing it is offered medications, or behavioral interventions, or disability accommodations, or simply abandonment. What they are not offered is what every shamanic culture in history knew they needed: *recognition that what is happening to them is a transformation, not a malfunction, and that there is a role for them on the other side of it.*

This is the wound that empire inflicts, and it's the wound that hasn't healed. But the story doesn't end here — because the specialist's capacity is persistent, and even within the long night of ontic civilization, it has found ways to survive. The anabolic cascade is powerful, but it's not total. Catabolic processes — the breaking-down, the composting, the return to source — continue in the margins, in the cracks, in the institutions that imperial culture tolerates because it doesn't quite understand what they're doing.

Some of those survival strategies were deliberate. Some were the kind of lucky accident that looks, in retrospect, like the spirits were still paying attention.

We turn next to the distinction between religion and spirituality — between the ontic administrator and the phenomenal practitioner operating in disguise — and to the peculiar institutions that served as vehicles for smuggling the specialist's gifts through the long imperial night. Monasteries. Mystery schools. Artistic lineages. The places where the shaman wore a monk's robe, and the drum became a prayer wheel, and the specialist's perception survived by learning to speak the empire's language while meaning something else entirely.
